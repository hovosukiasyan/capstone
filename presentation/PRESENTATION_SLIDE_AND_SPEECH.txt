WHAT TO SAY (≈ 60 sec):
"Armenia's poverty statistics work like this: ArmStat surveys households, crunches
numbers, and publishes one poverty rate per region, once a year. By the time that
number is public, it's already describing last year. Any policy response is
already late.

My project tries to close that gap with two data sources. First, the ILCS 2015
household survey — over 5,000 households with 29 features after cleaning —
where I build ML models to understand what predicts household income at the
micro level. One non-obvious challenge here: the interview month was not directly
available in the dataset and had to be reverse-engineered from household IDs,
yielding exactly 432 households per month — an important seasonality control.

Second, the ArmStat regional panel covering all 11 marzes from 2016 to 2022 —
seven annual observations per region — where I build time-series models to
forecast next year's poverty rate.

The hardest constraint in this project is that seven data points is genuinely
not a lot. Neural networks that normally need hundreds of observations fail
badly here. I had to be honest about that, and let the simplest models win."

WHAT TO SAY (≈ 60 sec):
"The project has two modelling strands.

The first is a household income model on ILCS 2015. I trained five supervised
regressors and used Bayesian optimization with Optuna to tune the top three.
The best model — Gradient Boosting after tuning — achieves an R-squared of 0.36
on the held-out test set. That's modest, but consistent with the literature on
welfare proxies from single survey waves. The clearest finding from feature
importance: households with diversified income sources are significantly richer.
The single variable income_source_count explains more variance than any asset
or housing quality variable.

The second strand is regional time-series forecasting. I benchmarked four neural
architectures against classical baselines at yearly, monthly, and daily
granularity. But I want to be direct: the monthly and daily metrics look
artificially good because I forward-filled annual values to create them. There
is no genuine new information — just near-perfect autocorrelation by construction.
The only honest benchmark is yearly.

And on yearly data, a zero-parameter Lag-1 baseline — just predicting next year
equals this year — beats every neural network. That's what seven data points
buys you.

The real test of all of this is the 2022 holdout backtest. I trained on 2016
through 2021, sealed away 2022, and only looked at it once. That's coming next."

WHAT TO SAY (≈ 60 sec):
"Here is the primary result. Three models, trained exclusively on 2016 to 2021,
evaluated against the actual 2022 poverty rates that ArmStat published.

Lag-1 — the simplest possible model, no parameters whatsoever — achieves a
regional MAE of 5.07 percentage points across all 11 marzes. That's the best
regional accuracy. Ridge AR, which uses lag features and a stress index built
from crime rates and hospital density, does worse regionally but compensates
elsewhere. When you average them 50/50 into an Ensemble, something interesting
happens at the national level: Lag-1 tends to over-predict recovering regions,
Ridge tends to under-predict them, and those directional biases cancel under
population weighting.

The result: the Ensemble predicts Armenia's 2022 national poverty rate at 24.28%.
The actual number published by ArmStat was 24.3%. That's a 0.02 percentage point
error — effectively zero.

Not every region is that clean — Gegharkunik and Vayots Dzor are hard to predict
because of year-over-year swings that no model captures well with only seven
data points. That's an honest limitation I document explicitly.

I want to be clear that this result is not lucky. It's the product of a
carefully designed validation setup: trained on one window, tested blind on
another. The methodology is honest, not just the result.

Now let me show you where all of this lives."

Q1: "Your R² is only 0.36 on the household model. Isn't that quite low?"
→  "Yes, and I say so explicitly. Single-wave cross-sectional surveys routinely
    achieve R² in the 0.3–0.5 range for income prediction because unobserved
    heterogeneity — family networks, informal income, luck — isn't in the data.
    The value here isn't the absolute R²; it's identifying which features matter
    (income_source_count is far ahead of the rest) and doing so with a rigorous
    held-out test rather than just training performance."

Q2: "Why not use more recent data — after 2022?"
→  "ArmStat's regional poverty estimates for 2023 were not publicly available
    at the time of data collection. The 2022 holdout is the most recent data
    available, and using it as a test set rather than training data is the
    methodological choice that gives the validation credibility. When 2023
    data is released, the next step is automated ingestion and re-running
    the validation script."

Q3: "The neural networks performed worse than Lag-1. Was it worth building them?"
→  "Yes, for two reasons. First, it confirms empirically — not just theoretically —
    that complexity doesn't help when you have 7 data points per region. That
    finding needed to be demonstrated, not assumed. Second, the monthly- and
    daily-granularity experiments confirmed the implementation was correct:
    the GRU hits near-perfect monthly R² as expected on interpolated data,
    showing the pipeline works. The lesson — that inflated metrics on
    forward-filled data are not evidence of better forecasting — is one of the
    most practically important findings in the project."

Q4: "What's the stress index and why did you use it?"
→  "The stress index is a composite score built from crime rate per 100k,
    hospitals per 100k, and population. It's a rough proxy for structural
    socioeconomic pressure at the marz level. Ridge AR uses lag features of
    both poverty rate and the stress index. The idea was that regions with
    deteriorating infrastructure or rising crime might show poverty increases
    that a pure lag on poverty alone would miss. In practice, the stress index
    didn't help Ridge beat Lag-1 regionally, but it contributed to the ensemble
    by pulling predictions in a different direction — which is why the national
    error cancels so cleanly."

Q5: "How did you recover the interview month from ILCS?"
→  "The public-use file didn't expose interview month directly as a column.
    I reverse-engineered it from the household ID structure and survey wave
    documentation, which yielded exactly 432 households per month — a
    perfectly balanced distribution that confirmed the recovery was correct.
    Without this, seasonal income effects would have been an uncontrolled
    confound in the household model."

Q6: "What would you do with more data or more time?"
→  "Three things. One: extend the ArmStat panel as new annual data is released —
    each new year materially helps the neural models. Two: add bootstrap
    confidence bands to the 2023–2026 projections so users can see the
    uncertainty range, not just point forecasts. Three: build a scenario
    slider interface where users can adjust macroeconomic inputs and see the
    projected poverty impact — turning the dashboard from an analytics tool
    into a policy simulation tool."

Q7: "Is the 0.02 percentage point national error just good luck?"
→  "No — and the rolling validation shows why. The ensemble's near-zero
    national error comes from a structural property: Lag-1 systematically
    over-predicts recovering regions, Ridge under-predicts them, and population
    weighting causes those biases to cancel at the national level. This is
    consistent across validation years, not a one-time coincidence. What the
    rolling validation does show is year-to-year variance — the 2021 Ridge
    spike to 22pp MAE is a reminder that no model is robust to structural
    outliers in small panels."
